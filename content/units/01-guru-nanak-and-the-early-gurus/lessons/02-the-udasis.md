After his experience at the Bein, Guru Nanak Dev Ji did not stay in Sultanpur Lodhi. He set out on a series of long journeys known as the [[udasi|udasis]]. Tradition describes four main journeys, east, south, north and west, which took him across much of South Asia and beyond.

## A companion with a rabab

He did not travel alone. Bhai Mardana, a Muslim musician from Talwandi who had known him since childhood, went with him and played the [[rabab|rabab]] while the Guru sang. Their partnership was itself a message: a Hindu-born teacher and a Muslim musician, making music together about one Creator.

:::

Guru Nanak Dev Ji taught through song. Many of the hymns he composed on these journeys were later collected in Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji, and they are still sung in every [[gurdwara|gurdwara]] today.

## Questions, not lectures

The janamsakhis remember him less for long sermons than for simple actions that made people think.

At Hardwar, on the Ganges, he saw pilgrims throwing water towards the rising sun as an offering to their ancestors. He began throwing water in the opposite direction. When people asked what he was doing, he said he was watering his fields in Punjab. If their water could reach the sun, his could surely reach a few hundred miles.

In the west, tradition says he travelled as far as Mecca. When he lay down to rest with his feet towards the Kaaba, a man angrily told him to move. He replied by asking the man to turn his feet in a direction where God was not.

## Bhai Lalo and Malik Bhago

At Saidpur, now Eminabad in Pakistan, he chose to stay with Bhai Lalo, a poor carpenter, instead of accepting a feast from Malik Bhago, a rich official. In the story, the Guru takes bread from each of them and squeezes it. Milk flows from Bhai Lalo's bread, earned by honest work, and blood from Malik Bhago's, earned by exploiting others.

:::figure id="janamsakhi-bhai-lalo"
:::

The story puts [[kirat-karni|kirat karni]], honest living, at the centre of what it means to be religious.

## Witness to an invasion

While Guru Nanak Dev Ji was in Saidpur, the town was attacked by the army of Babur, who would go on to found the Mughal Empire. The Guru described the suffering he saw in a set of hymns known as the Babarvani, which are part of Sri Guru Granth Sahib Ji. They are among the earliest eyewitness responses to Babur's invasion, and they speak openly against the cruelty of rulers.

:::perspectives
The overall picture of long journeys is widely accepted, but the details are debated. The janamsakhis disagree about the order, length and destinations of the udasis, and some of the farthest journeys, such as those to Mecca, Baghdad and Sri Lanka, are known mainly from tradition. Historians such as W.H. McLeod have argued that we cannot confirm many specific places. Others point to long-standing local traditions and gurdwaras at sites associated with the Guru as evidence that his travels were remembered across a very wide area.
:::
